Routine screening blood tests by age

Screening means testing people without symptoms to catch problems early. These are the lab-based screens US guidelines recommend for average-risk adults, and the popular tests they don't recommend routinely.

By the Lab.Direct editorial teamUpdated November 11, 20268-minute read

Guidelines below are for average-risk adults. Family history, pregnancy, medicines and conditions such as diabetes or kidney disease change what and how often you should test. Use this as a starting point for a conversation with a clinician.

Once for every adult

  • Hepatitis C: the CDC recommends screening at least once for all adults 18 and older; the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommends screening adults 18–79.
  • HIV: the CDC recommends testing everyone 13–64 at least once; the USPSTF recommends screening ages 15–65.
  • Hepatitis B: the CDC recommends screening all adults 18 and older at least once using a three-part blood test.
  • Lipoprotein(a): not a USPSTF recommendation, but European Atherosclerosis Society guidance suggests measuring it at least once in adulthood because levels are mostly inherited.

By decade

20s and 30sCholesterol (lipid panel) from age 20, every 4–6 years if normal (American Heart Association). Chlamydia and gonorrhea yearly for sexually active women 24 and younger. Syphilis and other STI testing with increased risk. HIV and hepatitis C once.

45–54Colorectal cancer screening from 45; a yearly FIT stool test is one recommended option. Continue diabetes and cholesterol screening.
55–69Men: decide with a clinician whether to have PSA screening for prostate cancer. Continue colorectal, diabetes (to age 70) and cholesterol screening.
70–75Continue colorectal screening to 75. PSA screening is not recommended from 70. Cholesterol-related decisions focus on statin use for prevention (USPSTF covers 40–75).
Over 75Screening becomes individual, based on health and life expectancy.

Extra testing with risk factors

  • Diabetes or high blood pressure: yearly kidney checks — creatinine with eGFR and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio — plus A1c every 3–6 months if you have diabetes.
  • Family history of early heart disease: lipid panel earlier and more often; consider ApoB and Lp(a).
  • Heavy periods, plant-based diet or frequent blood donation: CBC and ferritin.
  • New or multiple sexual partners: periodic STI screening.
  • Long-term metformin or acid-reducing medicine, or age over 60: vitamin B12.

Popular tests that aren't recommended for routine screening

For people without symptoms or risk factors, the USPSTF has found insufficient evidence to recommend routine screening for vitamin D deficiency or thyroid disease. That doesn't mean the tests are useless — they're valuable when there's a reason, such as symptoms, medicines or risk factors — but testing everyone hasn't been shown to improve health and can lead to unnecessary follow-up.
