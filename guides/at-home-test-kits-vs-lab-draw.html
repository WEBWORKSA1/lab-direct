At-home test kits vs. a lab blood draw

Mail-in kits make testing easy, but not every test works well from a few drops of blood. Here's how to decide.

By the Lab.Direct editorial teamUpdated November 11, 20266-minute read

Two very different kinds of "home test"

Home-use tests give you a result at home, like a pregnancy test or a rapid COVID-19 test. Home collection kits let you collect a sample — finger-prick blood, saliva, urine, a swab or stool — and mail it to a laboratory that runs the test and posts results online. This guide is about collection kits, which cover most "at-home lab tests" sold online.

How accurate are collection kits?

The analysis happens in a clinical laboratory, so the chemistry can be just as accurate as a lab draw. The weak point is the sample. Capillary blood from a fingertip can differ slightly from vein blood, small samples limit how many tests can run, and squeezing the finger can break red cells and distort results. Samples also travel for days, so tests sensitive to time and temperature are less suitable. Rejected or insufficient samples are the most common problem; good providers offer a free recollection.

Tests that work well at home

  • Hemoglobin A1c
  • Lipid panel (from validated finger-prick devices)
  • TSH and some thyroid panels
  • Vitamin D
  • STI panels using urine or self-collected swabs
  • HIV and hepatitis C screening
  • FIT stool test for colorectal cancer screening

Tests better done at a lab

  • Complete blood count — cell counts need fresh, properly preserved blood.
  • Metabolic panels — potassium is easily distorted by hemolysis and shipping.
  • Iron studies and fasting tests that depend on precise timing.
  • PT/INR and other clotting tests — they need special tubes and fast processing (people on warfarin may use dedicated home meters prescribed by their clinic).

Tips for a good finger-prick sample

  1. Drink water and warm your hands under warm water for a few minutes.
  2. Stand, let your arm hang down and swing it gently to bring blood to your fingers.
  3. Use the side of a middle or ring fingertip, not the pad.
  4. Let drops form and fall into the collector; don't squeeze hard or "milk" the finger.
  5. Fill to the line, label it, and post it the same day, avoiding weekends if the kit says so.

Choosing a kit

Look for testing in CLIA-certified (and ideally CAP-accredited) laboratories, a physician who reviews abnormal results, clear sample-failure and refund policies, and transparent pricing including shipping. Kits for some tests, such as cancer screening, may require FDA authorization or a prescription.
