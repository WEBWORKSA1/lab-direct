How to prepare for a blood test

Most blood tests need no preparation at all. A few are thrown off by a meal, a supplement, a workout or the time of day. Here's how to get an accurate result the first time.

By the Lab.Direct editorial teamUpdated November 11, 20267-minute read

Do you need to fast?

Only some tests require fasting. The common ones are fasting glucose, fasting insulin and iron studies. Many labs also ask for a fast before a comprehensive metabolic panel so the glucose value is a fasting one. Cholesterol testing used to require a fast, but current guidance accepts non-fasting lipid panels for most screening; you'll be asked to fast if a previous triglyceride result was high or your clinician wants a fasting value.

If your order says "fasting", that usually means 8–12 hours with nothing to eat or drink except water. In practice:

  • Water is allowed and encouraged.
  • Skip coffee and tea, even black. Caffeine and the drink itself can nudge results.
  • Skip gum, mints, juice, milk, alcohol and smoking or vaping.
  • Book an early-morning appointment so most of the fast happens while you sleep.
  • Eat as soon as the draw is done; bring a snack if you tend to feel faint.
If you take insulin or another medicine that lowers blood sugar, ask your clinician how to adjust it before a fasting test. Fasting without adjusting can cause low blood sugar.

Medicines

Keep taking prescribed medicines unless your clinician tells you otherwise. A few exceptions matter:

  • Thyroid medicine: if the test is monitoring your dose, many clinicians prefer you take levothyroxine after the draw so the level reflects your steady state.
  • Blood thinners: for a PT/INR, take warfarin as usual and tell the lab your dose and timing.
  • Steroids and hormones: steroid tablets, creams and inhalers, and birth control pills affect cortisol and some hormone results. Tell the lab what you use.
  • Testosterone therapy: timing relative to your dose changes the level. Follow your prescriber's instructions.

Supplements, especially biotin

Biotin (vitamin B7), common in hair, skin and nail supplements and some multivitamins, can interfere with lab tests that use biotin-based chemistry, including some thyroid, hormone and cardiac tests. The US Food and Drug Administration has warned that high doses can cause falsely high or falsely low results. Tell the lab and your clinician if you take biotin, and ask whether and how long to pause it before testing.

Other supplements matter for the test they target. If you take iron, skipping it for 24 hours before an iron panel gives a truer picture of your baseline. For vitamin D or B12, keep your usual routine steady so the result reflects what you normally take.

Alcohol, exercise and meals the day before

  • Alcohol: avoid it for at least 24 hours. It can raise triglycerides and liver enzymes such as GGT.
  • Hard exercise: avoid intense workouts for 24–48 hours. Muscle strain raises AST, CK and sometimes creatinine, and can make healthy kidneys or liver look stressed.
  • Big meat meals: a large serving of cooked meat can raise creatinine for several hours.
  • Sexual activity and cycling: avoid ejaculation for 48 hours and long bike rides for a day before a PSA test.

Timing matters for some tests

TestBest timing or preparation
Fasting glucose, fasting insulinMorning, after at least 8 hours of fasting
Iron panelMorning, fasting; skip iron supplements for 24 hours
TestosteroneAbout 7–10 a.m.; confirm a low result with a second morning test
CortisolAbout 7–9 a.m., unless your clinician orders a different time
PSANo ejaculation for 48 hours; before, not after, a prostate exam
Celiac screenKeep eating gluten daily for several weeks beforehand
Urine testsFirst-morning, clean-catch midstream sample where possible
Estradiol and other cycle hormonesOn the cycle day your clinician specifies

Hydration and the draw itself

Drink a glass or two of water in the hour before your appointment unless told otherwise. Well-hydrated veins are easier to find, and dehydration can concentrate some results. Wear sleeves that roll up easily. If you've fainted during a blood draw before, tell the phlebotomist; you can have blood taken lying down.

What to bring

  • Photo ID
  • Your lab order (requisition), printed or on your phone
  • Insurance card, if you're using insurance
  • A list of medicines and supplements
  • A snack and drink for after a fasting test

After the draw

Keep pressure on the site for a few minutes and leave the bandage on for an hour or so. A small bruise is common. Seek care if your arm becomes very swollen, numb or painful. Results for routine tests usually arrive in one to three days; if a value is critical, the lab contacts the ordering provider directly.
