Direct-access lab testing, explained

You can order many common blood tests yourself, pay a posted cash price and see results online, usually without visiting a doctor first. Here's how it works and where its limits are.

By the Lab.Direct editorial teamUpdated November 11, 20267-minute read

How it works

  1. Choose a test or panel on a direct-access provider's website and pay the listed price.
  2. A physician order is generated. Most providers have an independent licensed physician review the request and sign the lab order (requisition). Some states allow consumers to order certain tests without any physician involvement.
  3. Give a sample. Walk in or book at a patient service center of a national or regional lab network, or use an at-home collection kit and mail it back.
  4. Get results online, usually within one to three business days for routine tests. Many providers include a physician review of abnormal results or offer a telehealth consult.

Why people use it

  • Known price up front. Cash prices for common tests can be lower than what an insured patient with a high deductible pays.
  • Speed and convenience. No wait for an appointment just to get an order.
  • Control and privacy. Results come straight to you, and testing isn't billed to insurance.
  • Tracking. People monitoring a known condition, fitness or a supplement routine can retest on their own schedule.

Where it's available

Rules are set state by state in the US. Most states allow direct-access testing for at least some tests; a small number, most notably New York, have historically restricted or prohibited consumer-ordered testing. Providers check your state at checkout. In Canada, provincial plans cover medically necessary tests ordered by a clinician, while private labs offer self-pay testing for many tests; what you can order without a requisition varies by province and lab.

Limits and risks

  • Interpretation. A number without context can mislead. Plan who will review your results with you, especially abnormal ones.
  • Over-testing. Large panels raise the chance of a borderline flag in a healthy person, which can lead to anxiety and follow-up costs. Test for a reason.
  • Insurance. Self-paid tests usually aren't reimbursed and may not count toward your deductible, although HSA and FSA funds can generally be used.
  • Critical results. Ask how the provider handles a dangerously abnormal value. Good providers have a physician contact you promptly.
  • Medical records. Results may not flow into your clinician's records automatically; bring or upload them.

When direct access is the wrong choice

Don't use self-ordered testing in place of urgent care. Chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, sudden weakness or confusion, severe abdominal pain or heavy bleeding need emergency care, not a lab order.
  • Monitoring medicines that need dose changes, such as warfarin, lithium or chemotherapy, belongs with your treating team.
  • Pregnancy complications, symptoms of serious infection, or a suspected new serious diagnosis need a clinician.
  • Screening tests with complex follow-up, such as PSA, are best done after a conversation about benefits and harms.
