How to read your lab report

Lab reports are written for clinicians, but the logic is simple once you know where to look. This guide walks through each part of a typical report and how to think about a flagged value.

By the Lab.Direct editorial teamUpdated November 11, 20268-minute read

The parts of a report

ColumnWhat it tells you
Test nameThe analyte measured, sometimes abbreviated (for example ALT, eGFR, TSH). Panels list each component on its own line.
ResultYour value.
FlagH for high, L for low; some labs mark critical values with HH, LL, an asterisk or the word "critical".
Reference intervalThe range seen in most healthy people, set by that lab for that method.
UnitsHow the value is expressed, such as mg/dL or mmol/L.
Collection and report timesWhen the sample was drawn and when it was reported. Collection time matters for timed tests like cortisol.
CommentsNotes such as "hemolyzed sample", "non-fasting" or an interpretation for tests like A1c or eGFR.

What a reference range really means

Most reference intervals are built to include the middle 95% of results from a healthy reference group. That means about 1 in 20 healthy people will fall outside the range on any single test, purely by chance. Run 20 independent tests and the chance that at least one is flagged in a perfectly healthy person is about 64% (1 − 0.95²⁰). A single, slightly abnormal value is therefore common and often isn't meaningful on its own.

Some markers use decision limits instead of a population range. Glucose, A1c, cholesterol and vitamin D are judged against cut-offs linked to health outcomes, such as an A1c of 6.5% for the diabetes range. That's why a "normal" range for LDL cholesterol may look lower than what many healthy people actually have.

Your own lab's reference range always takes precedence. Ranges vary between labs and methods, and they're different for children, during pregnancy and sometimes for older adults.

Flags and critical values

An H or L flag simply means the value is outside the lab's range. It's not a diagnosis. Critical values are different: they're far enough outside the range to be potentially dangerous, so the lab phones the ordering provider immediately. Examples include very high or low potassium, very low glucose and very low platelets. If you see a critical flag and haven't heard from a clinician, call the ordering provider or the lab the same day.

Units and conversions

US labs mostly report conventional units; much of the world uses SI units. Before comparing with a chart or our decoder, make sure the units match.

MarkerConversion
Glucosemg/dL ÷ 18 = mmol/L
Total, LDL and HDL cholesterolmg/dL ÷ 38.67 = mmol/L
Triglyceridesmg/dL ÷ 88.57 = mmol/L
Creatinineµmol/L ÷ 88.4 = mg/dL
Vitamin D (25-hydroxy)nmol/L ÷ 2.5 = ng/mL
A1c% and mmol/mol are different scales; 6.5% ≈ 48 mmol/mol

Read patterns, not single numbers

Clinicians interpret results together. A few classic patterns:

  • Iron deficiency: low hemoglobin, small red cells (low MCV) and low ferritin.
  • B12 or folate deficiency: low hemoglobin with large red cells (high MCV).
  • Underactive thyroid: high TSH with low free T4.
  • Liver irritation: raised ALT and AST; raised alkaline phosphatase with bilirubin points toward the bile ducts instead.
  • Dehydration: BUN, creatinine, sodium and albumin all nudged up together.
  • Insulin resistance: high triglycerides, low HDL and fasting glucose creeping toward 100 mg/dL.

Trends beat snapshots

A result that has been stable for years at the edge of the range is usually less concerning than a value that moved sharply since last time, even if the new value is still "normal". Keep copies of your reports, ideally from the same lab, so you can compare like with like.

Things that can skew a result

  • Hemolysis (red cells breaking during the draw) falsely raises potassium and some enzymes.
  • Recent meals raise glucose and triglycerides.
  • Hard exercise raises AST, CK and creatinine.
  • Biotin supplements can distort some thyroid and hormone tests.
  • Acute illness raises CRP, ferritin and white cells and can temporarily change thyroid results.
  • Time of day changes cortisol, testosterone and iron.

Questions to ask your clinician

  1. Which results matter most for me, and why?
  2. Could anything about how or when I was tested explain a flagged value?
  3. Should we repeat this test, and when?
  4. What would change our plan, and what can I do in the meantime?
